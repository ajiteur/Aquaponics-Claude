# First-slice basil wholesale economics input pack

## Scope and geography choice

India and the Netherlands are both worth keeping in the first slice, but for different reasons. India gives you a plausible lower-labour and lower-electricity operating context with meaningful upside if basil can reliably move through a premium fresh-herb channel. The Netherlands gives you a much cleaner market structure for a `basil bulk kg wholesale` path because Dutch fresh-herb trade is explicitly documented at importer/wholesaler level, the country is a major herb logistics hub, and labour and electricity inputs are available from official statistical and sectoral sources. On evidence quality alone, the Netherlands is the cleaner benchmarking case; on potential unit-cost advantage, India remains strategically important. citeturn38view2turn11view2turn4view3turn34view0turn25view0

I do **not** recommend adding a third geography in this pass. The biggest uncertainty is not “which extra country to include”, but “how precisely the first wholesale transaction point is defined for basil in each of the two required geographies.” A third country would add breadth faster than it would improve decision quality. The better next step is a tighter stage-matching refresh for basil price evidence inside India and the Netherlands. citeturn38view1turn34view0

For secondary cross-geo normalisation only, I use the ECB reference rate for **20 April 2026** of **EUR 1 = INR 109.4525**. These conversions are for comparison only, not for canon. citeturn43search1

## India input pack

### Price band

The strongest directly evidenced India-side basil price I found is from a 2025 open-access hydroponic basil economics study in Andhra Pradesh. The paper reports **238 kg** of basil output, a **sale price of ₹1,000/kg**, and **gross return of ₹238,000** for a 250 m² naturally ventilated polyhouse NFT setup. That is useful as a **hydroponic fresh-basil selling-price anchor**, but it is **not clean proof of first-wholesale bulk trade**; it is better treated as an upper anchor for a premium fresh-herb path than as a canonical wholesale price. citeturn35view0

Because the evidence is stage-unclear, the most reviewable first-slice price pack for India is:

| Field | Recommended working value | Market level used | Evidence type | Confidence | Why |
|---|---:|---|---|---|---|
| Conservative price | **₹600/kg** | operator-adjusted first wholesale | operator assumption informed by hydroponic premium anchor | Low | 40% haircut from the study’s ₹1,000/kg to avoid treating a study sale assumption as a universal wholesale fact |
| Base price | **₹800/kg** | operator-adjusted first wholesale | hydroponic analog + operator assumption | Low-Medium | Keeps the study anchor in play, but discounts for stage ambiguity |
| Optimistic price | **₹1,000/kg** | seller-side realised price in study | hydroponic analog | Medium | Directly evidenced in the 2025 Andhra basil hydroponic paper |

At the ECB rate above, that is roughly **€5.48/kg**, **€7.31/kg**, and **€9.14/kg** respectively. Again, those euro values are comparison-only. citeturn35view0turn43search1

### Labour-rate band

India’s labour evidence is mixed, and that is one of the most important findings in this research.

The same Andhra basil hydroponic study budgets labour at **288 days × ₹250/day = ₹72,000/year**. That is a usable observation of **direct cash labour as costed by that paper**, but it is too weak to promote as a burdened commercial labour rate. citeturn35view0

A stronger formal-compliance floor comes from the Chief Labour Commissioner’s April 2025 agriculture wage order. It gives area-wise minimum wages including VDA for agriculture of **₹465–₹514/day for unskilled workers** and **₹515–₹610/day for skilled/clerical workers**. citeturn25view0

On top of wage rates, formal employers may face employer-side contributions. EPFO states that both employee and employer contribute **12%** of basic wages plus dearness allowance, subject to the statutory framework and wage ceiling, and ESIC states the employer contribution rate is **3.25%**. citeturn27search1turn27search0turn26search1

That supports the following **burdened commercial production labour** pack for India:

| Field | Recommended working value | Local basis | Secondary comparison | Evidence type | Confidence |
|---|---:|---|---:|---|---|
| Conservative labour | **₹703/day** | ₹610/day skilled floor × 1.1525 | **€6.42/day** | minimum wage + statutory burden proxy | Medium |
| Base labour | **₹602/day** | midpoint-type working value | **€5.50/day** | minimum wage + statutory burden proxy | Medium |
| Optimistic labour | **₹536/day** | ₹465/day unskilled floor × 1.1525 | **€4.89/day** | minimum wage + statutory burden proxy | Medium |

**Important caution:** the hydroponic basil study’s **₹250/day** should be kept as an **observed direct-labour accounting assumption**, not as the canonical burdened labour rate. It materially understates a compliance-oriented commercial labour model. citeturn35view0turn25view0turn27search1turn26search1

### Electricity-cost band

India’s electricity evidence is weaker than labour and price evidence, but there are still usable anchors.

The Tamil Nadu Electricity Regulatory Commission’s 2025 one-page revised tariff statement shows **HT Tariff I** with an **energy charge of ₹7.50 per unit** and **demand charge of ₹608/kVA/month**. That gives you a documented lower-anchor energy rate and, just as importantly, confirms that commercial production electricity can include **both per-kWh and demand-charge components**. citeturn20search4

The Central Electricity Authority’s annual tariff compilation explains that effective rates are computed from state tariff orders and assumed consumption levels, which reinforces that India does **not** have a single clean greenhouse-electricity tariff; category, state, and sanctioned load matter. BESCOM and other state tariff material also show commercial/industrial category splits and time-of-day treatment. citeturn13search2turn13search4turn20search2turn22search8

For first-slice modelling, the safest working position is:

| Field | Recommended working value | Basis | Evidence type | Confidence |
|---|---:|---|---|---|
| Conservative electricity | **₹9.5/kWh** | scenario allowance for category/state spread above documented HT anchor | policy/tariff source + provisional spread | Low |
| Base electricity | **₹8.5/kWh** | first-pass operating midpoint | policy/tariff source + provisional spread | Low |
| Optimistic electricity | **₹7.5/kWh** | documented HT anchor | policy/tariff source | Medium |

At the ECB comparison rate, this is about **€0.087/kWh**, **€0.078/kWh**, and **€0.069/kWh** respectively. citeturn20search4turn13search2turn43search1

### Packaging and handling decision

For the **first slice only**, I would **exclude dedicated packaging cost as a canonical default** in India for `fresh cut basil, bulk kg, non-retail-packed, first wholesale transaction point`. The reason is structural rather than optimistic: retail re-packing is explicitly out of scope, and for a short local wholesale path the relevant cost burden is more often **handling discipline, crate access, trimming, and losses**, not a mandatory retail pack format. citeturn38view1turn42search12

That said, outer pack and handling can become real quickly if the route is longer, export-facing, or quality-sensitive. CBI notes that fresh-herb pricing is materially affected by **handling, packing, and transport**, while re-packing is a separate price driver for small retail products. For this reason:

| Field | Recommended working value | Classification | Confidence |
|---|---:|---|---|
| First-slice default packaging cost | **₹0/kg** | common but deferrable | Medium |
| Optional route add-on | **separate scenario toggle** | channel-specific and not first-slice default | Medium |

I would not hard-code a per-kg packaging number for India until the specific wholesale route is known. citeturn38view1

### Saleability and shrink assumption

I did **not** find high-confidence India-specific basil saleability data for a bulk-kg wholesale path. This field should therefore remain explicitly non-canonical.

The science does justify caution: fresh basil profitability is limited by **short shelf life**, **poor handling**, **cold sensitivity**, and **microbial contamination**; UC Davis notes basil should be stored **above 10°C** and may keep for **1–2 weeks** under suitable conditions; Rutgers reports best storage near **55°F** and warns that basil held too cold blackens and loses aroma. citeturn42search1turn42search0turn42search3turn42search7

A cautious first-slice India assumption is:

| Field | Recommended working value | Classification | Confidence | Why |
|---|---:|---|---|---|
| Conservative saleability | **0.80** | operator assumption | Low | warm-chain and handling slippage can bite hard |
| Base saleability | **0.88** | operator assumption | Low | plausible short-chain disciplined handling case |
| Optimistic saleability | **0.92** | operator assumption | Low | good local handling without retail lag |

This should be stored as a **scenario assumption**, not as a universal biological constant. citeturn42search1turn42search0turn42search3

### High-confidence cautions

India has the largest **price-stage ambiguity** in this pack. The best basil-specific price anchor is a hydroponic study assumption/output at **₹1,000/kg**, but the clean first-wholesale stage is not proven. That makes India usable for scenario work, but not yet clean enough for a canonical pricing fact. citeturn35view0

The second major caution is that basil should **not** be handled like generic lettuce-style cold chain. Multiple strong sources indicate basil is damaged below about **10–12°C**, so a superficially “better refrigerated chain” can actually worsen quality if run too cold. In other words, price opportunity and cold-chain burden must be separated. citeturn42search0turn42search3turn42search4turn42search13

The third caution is electrical structure: if the facility lands in a tariff class with **demand charges**, low throughput or poor utilisation can make the apparent per-kWh tariff misleading for break-even per saleable kilogram. citeturn20search4turn13search2

## Netherlands input pack

### Price band

The strongest high-trust Netherlands-side basil price evidence I found is in the 2024 CBI market report for fresh culinary herbs. The report includes a table of **wholesale kilogram-box examples based on price lists of Dutch importing wholesalers**, with basil priced at **€7.50/kg from Kenya**, **€8.00/kg from Ethiopia**, and **€9.25/kg from Israel**. The key limitation is date: the table is explicitly for **March 2020**. That makes it structurally valuable but not current enough to be promoted to canon without a refresher. citeturn38view1

For the first slice, the most reviewable Dutch price pack is therefore:

| Field | Recommended working value | Market level used | Evidence type | Confidence | Why |
|---|---:|---|---|---|---|
| Conservative price | **€7.0/kg** | operator haircut on Dutch wholesaler examples | official market report + operator assumption | Low-Medium | keeps below the cited example range because the underlying examples are older |
| Base price | **€8.0/kg** | Dutch importing wholesaler level | official market report | Medium |
| Optimistic price | **€9.0/kg** | Dutch importing wholesaler level | official market report | Medium |

This is the cleanest Netherlands-side band I would use for a first working model, but I would label the whole field **provisional_pending_price_refresh**. citeturn38view1

### Labour-rate band

The Dutch greenhouse horticulture CAO gives directly useful production wage anchors. From **1 July 2025**, pay scale B2 is **€14.40/hour**, with scale C2 **€14.79**, D2 **€15.16**, E2 **€15.43**, and F2 **€16.07** for adults. citeturn4view3

Employer-side statutory burdens are not trivial. The Dutch Tax Administration shows 2026 rates of **AWf 2.74% or 7.74%**, **Aof 6.27% or 7.63%**, and **Wko 0.50%**, with additional differentiated Whk contributions. CBS also defines total labour costs per hour worked as including **gross wages, employer social contributions, holiday allowance, bonuses, and secondary employment benefits**. citeturn6view0turn6view1turn6view2

That supports this burdened production labour pack:

| Field | Recommended working value | Local basis | Evidence type | Confidence |
|---|---:|---|---|---|
| Conservative labour | **€19/hour** | higher wage scale + contribution/loading allowance | CAO + official employer-contribution structure | Medium |
| Base labour | **€17.5/hour** | mid burdened production rate | CAO + official employer-contribution structure | Medium |
| Optimistic labour | **€16/hour** | lower-end burdened rate | CAO + official employer-contribution structure | Medium |

This is not a claim that every basil worker in Dutch greenhouse horticulture costs exactly this amount. It is a practical first-pass burdened range derived from sector wage floors plus employer cost structure. citeturn4view3turn6view0turn6view1turn6view2

### Electricity-cost band

The Dutch electricity input is the strongest in the pack.

CBS reports, for **non-household electricity consumption of 500 to 2,000 MWh/year**, a **total price including VAT and tax** of **€0.254/kWh in 2024 H1**, **€0.255/kWh in 2024 H2**, **€0.252/kWh in 2025 H1**, and **€0.241/kWh in 2025 H2**. The same table shows **supply-only** figures of **€0.192–€0.194/kWh in 2024** and **€0.171–€0.182/kWh in 2025**, with the difference being network and related charges. citeturn11view2

For business-layer modelling, the cleanest first-slice choice is to use **delivered electricity cost**, not just commodity power, unless you are explicitly separating network charges elsewhere.

| Field | Recommended working value | Basis | Evidence type | Confidence |
|---|---:|---|---|---|
| Conservative electricity | **€0.26/kWh** | delivered total cost | official statistics | High |
| Base electricity | **€0.25/kWh** | delivered total cost | official statistics | High |
| Optimistic electricity | **€0.24/kWh** | delivered total cost | official statistics | High |

If you later split energy and network structurally, CBS already supports that separation. citeturn11view2

### Packaging and handling decision

As with India, I would **not** force a dedicated packaging cost into the first-slice default for `bulk kg wholesale`. But the Dutch case has a stronger warning around operational burden.

CBI describes the Netherlands as a major herb import, re-pack, and re-export hub. It also says fresh-herb final price is heavily influenced by **handling, packing, and transport**, and that re-packing is a major driver for small retail products. In the Netherlands, imported herbs are commonly redistributed to neighbouring countries, and the market is explicitly quality- and logistics-intensive. citeturn38view2turn3view0turn38view1

So the correct first-slice treatment is:

| Field | Recommended working value | Classification | Confidence |
|---|---:|---|---|
| First-slice default packaging cost | **€0/kg** | bulk/non-retail default | Medium |
| Route-specific add-on | **separate scenario toggle** | common but deferrable | Medium |

That keeps the first slice faithful to the product definition while preserving the fact that Dutch wholesale opportunities often sit inside a more demanding handling system. citeturn38view2turn38view1

### Saleability and shrink assumption

Again, there is no strong Netherlands-specific basil saleability number that I would trust as a canonical fact. This should remain a scenario input.

The reason to stay cautious is clear. CBI says fresh-herb quality and freshness are critical and buyer requirements are strict. UC Davis and Rutgers warn that basil is chilling sensitive and should be handled above standard herb cold-chain temperatures. A recent review also notes that basil’s profitability is constrained by short shelf life, poor handling, cold sensitivity, and microbial contamination. citeturn3view0turn42search0turn42search3turn42search1

A cautious Dutch first-slice assumption is:

| Field | Recommended working value | Classification | Confidence | Why |
|---|---:|---|---|---|
| Conservative saleability | **0.85** | operator assumption | Low | strict specs can convert damage into rejection |
| Base saleability | **0.90** | operator assumption | Low | disciplined wholesale handling case |
| Optimistic saleability | **0.95** | operator assumption | Low | good basil-specific handling above chilling range |

### High-confidence cautions

The Dutch fresh-herb channel is not “easy mode” just because it is organised. CBI is explicit that the Netherlands is a logistics and re-packing hub, and that freshness, handling, and strict requirements matter. That means Dutch price opportunity should always be read together with **handling burden**. citeturn38view2turn3view0

Basil’s temperature sensitivity is commercially important here. Sources converge that basil is injured below roughly **10–12°C**, and Rutgers cites **55°F** as optimal storage. This is a direct operating constraint in a temperate-country supply chain. citeturn42search0turn42search3turn42search4turn42search13

Electricity is also structurally burdensome in the Netherlands. Even before any lighting expansion logic, official non-household delivered prices around **€0.24–€0.26/kWh** are substantial compared with India’s plausible tariff anchors. citeturn11view2turn20search4

### Calculation-ready formulas

Use these formulas directly in the first slice:

```text
variable_cost_per_harvested_kg
  = direct_labour_cost_per_harvested_kg
  + electricity_cost_per_harvested_kg
  + packaging_handling_cost_per_harvested_kg
  + other_variable_cost_per_harvested_kg

variable_cost_per_saleable_kg
  = variable_cost_per_harvested_kg / saleability_rate

contribution_margin_per_saleable_kg
  = sale_price_per_saleable_kg - variable_cost_per_saleable_kg

break_even_saleable_kg
  = fixed_costs_for_period / contribution_margin_per_saleable_kg

required_harvested_kg
  = target_saleable_kg / saleability_rate
```

If you later model electricity more accurately, split it into:

```text
electricity_cost_for_period
  = (delivered_kWh * delivered_energy_rate)
  + demand_charges
  + fixed_connection_charges

electricity_cost_per_harvested_kg
  = electricity_cost_for_period / harvested_kg_for_period
```

That split is especially important in India, where tariff structures can include per-kWh energy charges **and** demand charges, and in the Netherlands, where CBS already shows a meaningful distinction between supply and network components. citeturn20search4turn11view2

## Working benchmark and deferred items

The **cleanest first working benchmark** is the **Netherlands**. That is not because it is the cheapest or “best”, but because the exogenous cost inputs are better documented and easier to audit: Dutch greenhouse wage floors are explicit, employer burden structure is explicit, non-household electricity prices are explicit, and the Netherlands’ role in fresh-herb wholesaling and redistribution is explicitly documented. citeturn4view3turn6view0turn6view1turn11view2turn38view2

India should still be kept as the second essential scenario, because it may offer much better labour- and power-side economics. But it has two unresolved problems for this first slice: the best basil price evidence is still not cleanly matched to the first-wholesale transaction stage, and the strongest labour evidence exposes a large gap between direct-cost assumptions in one basil paper and compliance-oriented wage floors. citeturn35view0turn25view0

### What should remain provisional or deferred

The following should remain **provisional** or **deferred**, not promoted to canon yet:

- **India price band** beyond the directly evidenced **₹1,000/kg** study anchor. The working ₹600/₹800/₹1,000 pack is usable for calculation, but only the top anchor is directly evidenced; the lower points are deliberate operator haircuts for stage ambiguity. citeturn35view0
- **India electricity upper band** above the documented **₹7.50/kWh** HT anchor. Keep it non-canonical until you pin the actual tariff class and state. citeturn20search4turn13search2
- **Both geographies’ saleability rates**. These are operator assumptions informed by postharvest science, not measured wholesale-path constants. citeturn42search1turn42search0turn42search3
- **Dedicated packaging cost** for the first slice. Because the product definition is explicitly bulk, non-retail-packed basil, packaging should stay out of the default model and enter only as a route-specific toggle. citeturn38view1
